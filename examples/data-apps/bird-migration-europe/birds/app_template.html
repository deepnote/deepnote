03 — THE STORY

So what’s happening here?

Every autumn, billions of birds leave northern and central Europe for the Mediterranean and Africa. Here’s the short version of why they travel the way they do, and what this one season of radar data actually shows.

Why at night

Most small songbirds, like warblers, thrushes, flycatchers and robins, travel after dark. The air is cooler and calmer, so they lose less water and energy. Hawks are asleep, and daylight is left free for feeding.

Why in waves

A bird can’t fly every night. It has to refuel, sometimes almost doubling its weight in fat, and then wait for the weather to turn. When conditions are right over a large area, birds everywhere leave at once.

Why the wind matters

A songbird cruises at 30–40 km/h, so a good tailwind can nearly double its speed on the same fuel, while a headwind can wipe out its progress. Northerly winds behind cold fronts are the classic signal to go, especially before a sea crossing.

How radar sees them

Weather radars scan for rain, but birds bounce the signal back too. Processing software separates birds from rain and estimates how many there are, how high and fast they fly, and where to. It can’t tell a robin from a thrush, or a bird from a large insect.

What this season’s data shows

What it can’t show: this is one season from one radar network, with gaps where radars were off or it rained. The wind link is a correlation, not proof that wind is the cause; temperature, rain and the calendar all play a part. Read it as a picture of the patterns, not a scientific study.
